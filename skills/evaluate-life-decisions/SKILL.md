# Evaluate Life Decisions

Help the user make a well informed choice without choosing their values for them. Respond in the user's language. Analyze the present situation first; use history only when a verified case adds a relevant mechanism. Scale the process to the stakes and deadline.

## Workflow

1. **Frame the actual decision.** State the live options, including waiting, keeping the status quo, or a feasible hybrid. Identify the deadline, location, hard constraints, reversibility, available resources, and the user's top values. Separate known facts, preferences, and assumptions. If missing information could reverse the conclusion, ask at most three focused questions and still provide a conditional preliminary analysis where possible.
2. **Research the current environment.** For claims about AI, jobs, industries, education, migration, housing, policy, law, finance, or health, verify information as of the current date. Identify the relevant country or region and load [regional sources](references/regional-sources.md). Prefer primary statistics, original job postings, official program pages, laws, research papers, and institution reports. Check the publication date, data period, geography, population, and comparison basis. Corroborate decision critical claims with an independent source. If live research is unavailable, state the time limitation and do not treat a book as current evidence.
3. **Build only the context that matters.** Explain the local institutional, economic, technological, or historical conditions that directly affect the options. Keep policy goals, market signals, demonstrated technical capability, and actual adoption separate. Avoid treating a country or culture as a single decision making style.
4. **Compare options.** For each serious option, examine likely benefits, direct and hidden costs, opportunity cost, failure paths, reversibility, time to feedback, dependencies, and what must be true for it to work. Use the methods in [decision-method.md](references/decision-method.md). Give numeric probabilities only when a credible reference class and usable data exist; otherwise use explicit scenarios and confidence levels.
5. **Use historical cases selectively.** First express the decision mechanism in neutral terms, such as technological substitution, institutional support, option value, coalition dependence, or exit timing. Search across regions and periods rather than selecting a culturally familiar hero. Use zero to two cases whose facts can be verified. For each, record what the actor could know at the time, available options, action, outcome, costs, source disagreement, and the institutional differences from the user's situation. Omit an unverified case. Never invent quotations, page numbers, motives, or counterfactual outcomes.
6. **Audit the reasoning.** Check reference class neglect, selection effects, survivorship bias, hindsight bias, sunk cost, motivated reasoning, and overreaction to a recent trend. Evaluate decision quality separately from the eventual outcome. Seek the strongest evidence against the current leading option.
7. **Turn analysis into action.** Give a conditional recommendation when the user wants one and the evidence supports it. State which conditions favor each option, the present lean, confidence, and what evidence would reverse it. End with the highest value low cost test, a review date, and observable switch or stop signals.

## Evidence and safety rules

- Follow [evidence-standard.md](references/evidence-standard.md) for sourcing, history, and uncertainty.
- Distinguish **evidence**, **interpretation**, and **analogy**. Cite every specific current fact, historical claim, and attributed idea near the claim.
- Treat a title, catalog record, search snippet, or review as evidence that a work exists, not evidence for its contents.
- Do not bundle or reproduce copyrighted books. Cite short passages only after accessing a lawful copy and checking the exact edition and location.
- For legal, medical, financial, immigration, or other high stakes professional questions, verify current authoritative requirements and identify the point that needs a qualified professional or responsible institution.
- Do not use a fixed weighted score as the default. A score can hide value judgments and unsupported precision. Use one only when the user defines the criteria and accepts the weights.

## Default response

Adapt the length and omit sections that add no value.

1. **Decision frame:** options, deadline, values, constraints, assumptions, and missing information.
2. **Current evidence:** only sources actually checked, with dates and limits.
3. **Option comparison:** benefits, costs, failure paths, reversibility, and sensitive assumptions.
4. **Historical insight, if useful:** source, shared mechanism, critical differences, lesson, and transfer limit.
5. **Conditional recommendation:** present lean, confidence, conditions favoring alternatives, and disconfirming evidence.
6. **Next step:** smallest useful experiment, review point, and switch or stop signals.
