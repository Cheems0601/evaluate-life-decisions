# Decision Method

Use only the tools that clarify the user's specific choice.

## Decision frame

Record:

- options that are actually available;
- deadline and time horizon;
- top values and nonnegotiable constraints;
- location and institutions involved;
- resources, dependencies, and commitments;
- reversible and irreversible parts;
- known facts, estimates, preferences, and open questions.

Do not invent a third option merely to appear creative. Include waiting or a staged option only when feasible.

## Reference classes

Choose the narrowest reference class with enough observations. Compare it with a broader class to detect selection bias. For education or career outcomes, consider field, country, institution type, career stage, visa or work authorization, and the period represented by the data.

Do not translate a population average directly into an individual's probability. Explain what makes the user differ from the reference class.

## Scenario comparison

For each option, test:

| Dimension | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Preconditions | What must be true for this option to work? |
| Upside | What meaningful outcome becomes possible? |
| Direct costs | What money, time, energy, and commitments are required? |
| Hidden costs | What skills, relationships, health, identity, or future options may erode? |
| Failure path | How is this likely to go wrong? |
| Reversibility | What can be recovered, and how long would recovery take? |
| Feedback | How soon will reality reveal whether the assumptions are sound? |
| Dependencies | Which people, institutions, policies, or technologies must cooperate? |

Use best supported, plausible upside, and plausible downside scenarios. Reserve “worst case” for a bounded credible outcome rather than an imaginable catastrophe.

## Probability and confidence

Use a numeric probability only when the event, time horizon, reference class, and evidence are defined. Otherwise use low, medium, or high confidence and state why. Do not assign percentages that merely restate intuition.

Separate:

- confidence that a fact is accurate;
- confidence that a causal explanation is right;
- confidence that an option fits the user's values;
- confidence in a forecast.

## Value of information

Prioritize an experiment when it can change the decision before the deadline at acceptable cost. Good tests resemble the uncertain part of the real option.

Examples include a short project with the target tools, conversations with several people one to three years ahead on the path, a realistic application, a temporary assignment, or a budget and visa eligibility check.

Specify:

1. the uncertain assumption;
2. the action that tests it;
3. the observation period;
4. the success, switch, or stop signal;
5. when the decision will be reviewed.

## Bias audit

Ask:

- Are vivid success stories replacing base rates?
- Did the sample exclude people who left or failed?
- Is a good result being confused with a good decision process?
- Is prior investment affecting the choice more than future value?
- Is the user collecting support for a preferred answer?
- Is a recent technology or news cycle being extrapolated too far?
- Does a historical analogy share the causal mechanism, or only the story shape?
